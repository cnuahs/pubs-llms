# Optical Imaging of Electrically Evoked Visual Signals in Cats: I. Responses to Corneal and Intravitreal Electrical Stimulation

Yan T. Wong, Student Member, IEEE, Luke E. Hallum, Spencer C. Chen, Student Member, IEEE, Norbert Dommel, Student Member, IEEE, Shaun L. Cloherty, Member, IEEE, John W. Morley, Gregg J. Suaning, Member, IEEE, Nigel H. Lovell, Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract—Microelectronic retinal prostheses have been shown to restore the perception of light to the blind through electrical stimulation. Conventional recording techniques such as recording electrode arrays on the visual cortex can give a basic understanding of the events that occur during such stimulation events, but their finite size and number limits the spatial resolution achievable with them. Optical imaging of intrinsic signals (OIS imaging) allows for greater resolution (approximately \(50 \mu \mathrm{m}\) ) of the activity in the cortex. This can be used to facilitate a greater understanding of the complex neurophysiological events that allow prosthetic vision. This paper shows responses to visual and electrical stimulation of the retina, and demonstrates that OIS imaging may be an effective technique in further refining stimulation techniques and implant designs for retinal prostheses.

## I. INTRODUCTION

A microelectronic retinal prosthesis aims to restore visual perception to those that have been blinded through the loss of photoreceptors in the eye, but have their remaining visual pathways intact. These prostheses electrically stimulate the surviving retinal tissue eliciting the sensation of phosphenes (spots of light) [1], [2]. Current retinal prostheses offer rudimentary vision with designs containing less than 100 electrodes. Roughly speaking, each electrode corresponds to a spot of light in the recipient's field of view (for review see [3]).

Understanding the neurophysiological underpinnings of prosthetic vision may assist in eliciting more stable percepts in implantees. This reliable elicitation, which is proving difficult to do, would increase the potential therapeutic benefit of an implant. Rizzo et al. [2] have reported mixed success in their human trials with percepts matching stimulation patterns \(48\%\) of the time for single electrode stimulation, and \(32\%\) of the time for multiple electrode stimulation.

Percepts were found to be reproducible approximately 66 % of the time.

This difficulty in reliably eliciting percepts may be due to the differences between normal sensory physiology and "prosthetic" sensory physiology. These differ in that, among other things, the latter (1) presumably involves the indiscriminate and simultaneous activation of all functional types of retinal ganglion cells; (2) the number of viable cells is reduced; (3) excitatory and inhibitory neuronal interplay that appeals to central visual templates is abolished; and, (4) there is an absence of spontaneous activity in the retina.

A method that complements electrophysiological measures of neural activity is the optical imaging of intrinsic signals (OIS imaging) [4], [5]. OIS imaging allows the measurement of cortical activation at a fine spatial scale (approximately 50 microns) over large areas of tissue (e.g., \(5 \mathrm{~mm} \times 5 \mathrm{~mm}\) ). OIS imaging utilizes a camera to measure the reflection of specific wavelengths of light as neural activation changes the levels of oxy- and deoxy- hemoglobin. This imaging technique can be used to investigate the differences in neural activation between normal vision and electronic vision.

This paper presents preliminary data of OIS imaging of the primary visual cortex in response to visual and electrical retinal stimulation in the anesthetized cat.

## II. METHODS

### A. Animal Preparation

Adult cats were used in this study. The cats were anesthetized through an induction dose of ketamine hydrochloride/xylazine hydrochloride \((25 \mathrm{mg} \mathrm{kg}^{- 1} / 1.5 \mathrm{mg} \mathrm{kg}^{- 1})\) , and given pre- medication of dexamethasone \((1.5 \mathrm{mg} \mathrm{kg}^{- 1} \mathrm{i.m.})\) to reduce cerebral oedema, and atropine sulphate \((0.2 \mathrm{mg} \mathrm{kg}^{- 1} \mathrm{s.c.})\) to reduce mucosal secretions. The femoral artery and vein were catheterized to allow blood pressure (and heart rate derived from the blood pressure trace) to be monitored, and to deliver an anesthetic (alfaxalone \(6 \mathrm{mg} \mathrm{kg}^{- 1} \mathrm{h}^{- 1}\) ) and paralyzing agent (gallamine \(5.6 \mathrm{mg} \mathrm{kg}^{- 1} \mathrm{h}^{- 1}\) ). A tracheotomy and bilateral pneumothorax were performed, and the animals were ventilated with \(70 \% \mathrm{N}_{2} \mathrm{O}\) / \(30 \% \mathrm{O}_{2}\) . A craniotomy was performed to reveal the primary visual cortex, and the dura mater was removed. The eyes were dilated through topical application of atropine sulphate \((10 \mathrm{mg} \mathrm{ml}^{- 1})\) , and phenylephrine hydrochloride

(25 mg ml \(^{- 1}\) ). Experiments were conducted with prior approval from the Animal Care and Ethics Committee at the University of New South Wales, and in accordance with the National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia guidelines for animal experimentation.

### B. Stimulation Techniques

Electrical stimulation was delivered to the animals through electrically isolated custom electronics. For both corneal and intravitreal stimulation, the electrodes were driven by the author's current retinal neurostimulator (for further details the reader is directed to [6] and [7]).

1) Corneal Stimulation: Two contact lens electrodes (ERG-jet, Micro Components, Switzerland) were placed on the eyes of the animals, with the return electrodes placed behind the ears on the ipsilateral side of each stimulating electrode. Balanced biphasic stimuli were presented with electrode shorting occurring in between stimulations to remove excess charge accumulation in the tissue. Pulse durations were varied from \(500\mu \mathrm{s}\) to \(1\mathrm{ms}\) , and currents were varied from \(600\mu \mathrm{A}\) to \(3.52\mathrm{mA}\) . The frequency of stimulation was varied from \(1\mathrm{Hz}\) to \(450\mathrm{Hz}\) , and bursts of stimuli lasted \(2 - 6\) seconds.

2) Intravitreal Stimulation: A \(500\mu \mathrm{m}\) platinum ball electrode was introduced into the vitreous of the left eye with the aid of a 19 gauge I.V. catheter. The stimulating electrode was placed in the middle of the vitreous, and the return electrode was placed on the back of the neck. Stimulation currents and pulse durations were varied from \(133\mu \mathrm{A}\) to \(3.52\mathrm{mA}\) , and \(100\mu \mathrm{s}\) to \(1\mathrm{ms}\) . The frequency of stimulation was varied from \(1\mathrm{Hz}\) to \(450\mathrm{Hz}\) , and bursts of stimuli lasted \(2 - 6\) seconds.

3) Visual Stimulation: Visual stimuli were presented to the animals in the form of drifting square-wave gratings on a \(20\mathrm{inch}\) monitor with a refresh rate of \(70\mathrm{Hz}\) (NEC Multisync P1250+, NEC Corp., Japan). The gratings had a spatial frequency of \(0.25\) cycles \(\mathrm{deg}^{-1}\) , and a drift rate of \(2\) cycles \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) . Stimuli were presented for \(4 - 8\) s at four orientations, \((0^{\circ},45^{\circ},90^{\circ},\) and \(135^{\circ}\) ).

### C. Recording Methods

1) Electrically Evoked Potentials: Electrically evoked potentials were recorded on the primary visual cortex using a platinum ball recording electrode \((2.5\mathrm{mm})\) , and an indifferent platinum electrode sutured subcutaneously, to the muscles in the neck of the animals. A PowerLab (ADInstruments Pty. Ltd., Australia) data acquisition system was used to record signals at \(40\mathrm{kHz}\) after a \(10000\) gain amplification and a low-pass filter at \(5\mathrm{kHz}\) and a high-pass filter at \(0.1\mathrm{Hz}\) (Model 1800 Microelectrode AC Amplifier, A-M Systems Inc., Washington, USA). Sixty-four runs were recorded, and averaged at a repeat rate of \(1\mathrm{Hz}\) for each stimulation run.

2) Optical Imaging: A CCD camera (Photometrics Cool-snap EZ Turbo 1394, Roper Scientific Inc., Arizona, USA) was focussed on the primary visual cortex, and triggered to image the cortex for \(15 - 40\mathrm{s}\) . The camera was focused \(800\mu \mathrm{m}\) below the surface of the cortex to approximately layer IV of the striate cortex. Images were recorded at \(4 - 8\) frames per second. Recordings were made with incident light provided by light emitting diodes with wavelengths of either \(615\mathrm{nm}\) (red) or \(530\mathrm{nm}\) (green). For further detail, the reader is directed to Hallum et al. [5]). Where possible, control sequences of no stimulation were interleaved with stimulation runs. Pre-stimulus times were monitored for a stable image mean intensity. Additionally, as an indication of neural activity, a composite mean intensity profile was monitored for a decrease in intensity at the onset of stimulation.

In the cat, neurons in layer IV of the primary visual cortex are organized into columns arranged radially to the cortical surface [8]. These columns, commonly referred to as the oc- . ular dominance columns, are approximately \(0.5\mathrm{mm}\) across and each receive afferent input from one eye. By stimulating one eye, visually or electrically, and then stimulating the other, the two different responses can be subtracted and then filtered to form ocular dominance maps, (regions of the striate cortex innervated by each eye). A filtering technique, Independent Component Analysis (ICA), which improves on standard ensemble averaging, was used to extract the elicited responses from the background noise (due to cardiac pulsation, respiratory movement, etc.). Further details on the filtering technique is presented in [9].

1) Visual Stimulation: OIS imaging of the visual cortex in response to visual stimuli is shown in Fig. 2. The intrinsic signals in response to the visual gratings were reliably elicited, and were of a sufficient magnitude for them to be imaged. Fig. 2(a) shows the area of the primary visual cortex being imaged (camera focussed at the surface), and Fig. 2(b) shows the ocular dominance columns (focussed \(800\mu \mathrm{m}\) below the surface).

As oxygen is consumed by neurons, and replenished by way of localized functional hyperemia, the light absorbed by tissue increases, resulting in darker patches in the areas of neuronal activation. The image illustrates how different patches on the cortex are activated by a single eye, with the dark patches representing cells receiving input from the left eye, and the light patches representing cells receiving input from the right eye. Fig. 2(c) shows the ocular dominance map after applying a spatial band- pass filter. This was done to better highlight the ocular dominance columns. Forty recordings were taken while stimulating the left eye only, and 40 recordings were taken stimulating the right eye only.

2) Corneal Stimulation: For corneal stimulation, the OIS imaging in response to biphasic, cathodic first stimuli of \(3.52\mathrm{mA}\) with \(1\mathrm{ms}\) cathodic and anodic pulse durations and a repeat frequency of \(450\mathrm{Hz}\) is shown in Fig. 3. Runs in which only the left eye was stimulated with the above parameters, were interleaved with right eye only stimulation to achieve the ocular dominance maps (Fig. 3(a)). Twenty recordings were completed for the left eye stimulation, and 20 recordings for the right eye stimulation. The areas of the cortex activated by the left eye are indicated by the darker patches, and the right eye by lighter patches. An ocular dominance map after spatial band-pass filtering is shown in Fig. 3(b).

OIS imaging of different current strengths with corneal stimulation was also recorded. Fig. 4 shows the differences in response to a \(3.52\mathrm{mA}\) stimulation (Fig. 4(a)) compared to a \(1.52\mathrm{mA}\) stimulation (Fig. 4(b)), (with all other stimulation parameters kept the same, pulse durations at \(1\mathrm{ms}\) and repeat frequency at \(450\mathrm{Hz}\) ). As expected, the lower current stimuli activates a much smaller area of the visual cortex (dark patches in Fig. 4(b)), and a significantly lower intensity of activation, but from the same location on the visual cortex.

3) Intravitreal Stimulation: For intravitreal stimulation, only the left eye of the animal was stimulated. Due to this, no ocular dominance maps are provided but a stimulus, control (no stimulus) difference image is shown in Fig. 5(a). This image has been spatially band-pass filtered (Fig. 5(b)) to highlight the regions of activation. The dark region of this image indicate the areas of activation for the left eye alone. Forty recordings were taken for the intravitreal stimulation, with each stimulation run interleaved with a control run in which no stimuli was delivered to the animal.

## IV. DISCUSSION

While we can reliably image visually elicited intrinsic signals and we can reliably record EEPs, we have experienced considerable difficulty in consistently imaging electrically evoked intrinsic signals. This latter signal may be of much attenuated amplitude as compared to its visual counterpart. This attenuation may be due to the atypical nature of prosthetic sensory physiology (as mentioned in the Introduction), and the consequent altered input to the cortex.

This apparent attenuation has made a systematic probing of the cortical response to electrical stimulation parameters (especially stimulation frequency) difficult. While we have shown imaged responses to high- frequency, high- current electrical stimuli, readers should note that this may not be an indication of the optimal stimulation parameters to elicit responses from the visual cortex. Indeed, we find the efficacy of high- frequency stimulation, as reported preliminarily here and elsewhere [10], somewhat surprising. Ngai et al. [11], for example, electrically stimulated rat sciatic nerve, and showed that the amplitude of the EEP falls monotonically with increasing stimulus frequency, however regional cerebral blood flow demonstrates a unimodal response, is proportional to integrated synaptic activity, and is maximal at \(5\mathrm{Hz}\) . The use of low frequencies is bourne out in subsequent OIS imaging studies in rats [12]. Our electrophysiological data show much attenuated EEPs in cat cortex at high frequencies, indicating integrated synaptic activity is low. These contradictory observations between the OIS imaging data for retinal prostheses and that reported for other electrical stimulation applications leave numerous open questions surrounding this model of retinal prosthesis.

## V. CONCLUSION

This paper demonstrates that OIS imaging can be used to measure neural activation in the visual cortex as a result of electrical activation of the retina, and hence assess the efficacy of a vision prosthesis. More importantly, OIS imaging can allow further investigation and understanding of the neurophysiological underpinnings of prosthetic vision. Additionally, a method to assess the retinotopy of epi- retinal electrical stimulation has been developed. The high spatial resolution achievable with this imaging technique coupled with multi- electrode arrays on the visual cortex, can be used to refine stimulation techniques and implant designs for a retinal vision prosthesis.
