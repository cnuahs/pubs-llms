# Focal Activation of Primary Visual Cortex Following Supra-choroidal Electrical Stimulation of the Retina: Intrinsic Signal Imaging and Linear Model Analysis

Shaun L. Cloherty, Markus A. Hietanen, Gregg J. Suaning and Michael R. Ibbotson

Abstract—We performed optical intrinsic signal imaging of cat primary visual cortex (Area 17 and 18) while delivering bipolar electrical stimulation to the retina by way of a supra-choroidal electrode array. Using a general linear model (GLM) analysis we identified statistically significant \((p < 0.01)\) activation in a localized region of cortex following supra-threshold electrical stimulation at a single retinal locus. Our results: (1) demonstrate that intrinsic signal imaging combined with linear model analysis provides a powerful tool for assessing cortical responses to prosthetic stimulation, and (2) confirm that supra-choroidal electrical stimulation can achieve localized activation of the cortex consistent with focal activation of the retina.

## I. INTRODUCTION

I. INTRODUCTIONMPLANTABLE microelectronic retinal prostheses represent a promising means of restoring sight to the blind. The conventional model of such devices includes an externally worn digital camera which samples the wearers visual environment. This stream of visual information is then processed before being conveyed wirelessly to an implanted neurostimulator connected to an array of electrodes placed within or adjacent to the retina. Electrical stimuli are thereby delivered to the surviving neurons of the retina to effect the perception of phosphenes.

We have previously described an information theoretic framework for the assessment of image processing strategies for use in retinal prostheses [1][2][3]. However, the physical and physiological constraints (discriminability of adjacent retinal stimuli or different stimulus intensities for example) for such strategies remain unclear. These constraints may be assessed through limited trials of prototype devices in blind human volunteers. However, for both practical and ethical reasons, assessment of the underlying physiological constraints may best be achieved first in animal models. Therefore, the development of animal models and means of observing and quantifying stimulus evoked responses is crucial for ensuring positive outcomes for implant recipients.

In this paper, we report our initial experience using optical intrinsic signal imaging of cat primary visual cortex, together with linear model analysis, as a means of assessing cortical activation elicited by a supra-choroidal retinal prosthesis.

## II. METHODS

All experimental procedures were approved by the institutional Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee (AEEC) at the Australian National University and were performed in strict compliance with the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes from the Australian National Health and Medical Research Council (NH&MRC).

### A. Animal Preparation for Imaging

Normally sighted juvenile cats (Felis catus; female, 3.0- 3.5 kg) were reared from birth in an outdoor enclosure subject to the prevailing night- day cycle. All animals were prepared for acute physiological recordings as described previously [4].

For imaging, the scalp was reflected and a small trephine hole \((10\mathrm{mm}\times 8\mathrm{mm}\) ; A4 to P6, L1 to L9) was made in the cranium, exposing the visual cortex (Areas 17 and 18) in the right hemisphere as shown schematically in Figure 1A. A stainless steel recording chamber was fixed to the cranium with dental acrylic and the dura mater removed. The chamber was then filled with silicone oil (Dow Corning 200, 50 cSt) and sealed with a glass cover slip, mechanically stabilizing the cortex for imaging.

### B. Ocular Surgery for Electrode Placement

B. Ocular Surgery for Electrode PlacementAn array of platinum stimulating electrodes as described below was introduced into the supra-choroidal space of the left eye by way of a small scleral tunnel. In brief, the globe was rotated to expose the tunnel site positioned between 12 and 1 o'clock (viewing the eye from the front). A small incision approximately half the thickness of the sclera in depth (typically \(0.5 - 1\mathrm{mm}\) ) and \(5\mathrm{mm}\) in width was then made in the sclera approximately \(7\mathrm{mm}\) posterior and parallel to the limbus. The tunnel was then extended \(2 - 3\mathrm{mm}\) posterior from this incision angling down to the surface of the choroid. The electrode array, loosely bound to a plastic foil introducer using viscoelastic gel, was then inserted through the tunnel using fine forceps and advanced into position close to the posterior pole of the eye (a distance of approximately \(19\mathrm{mm}\) ). The introducer was then removed and the protruding end of the electrode array was fixed to the exterior of the globe using fine sutures.

### C. Electrical Stimuli

C. Electrical StimuliElectrical stimuli consisted of trains of cathodic first charge balanced biphasic current pulses. Stimuli were delivered to the retina by way of an array of 14 planar platinum electrodes, as shown schematically in Figure 1C. The electrode was formed by laser patterning of platinum foil on a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate as described previously [5].

### D. Optical Intrinsic Signal Imaging

The exposed cortex was epi- illuminated using a custom built LED light source with a peak wavelength of \(520\mathrm{nm}\) . Images were acquired using a Dalsa 1M60P CCD camera fitted with a macroscope (formed using a pair of Nikkor \(50\mathrm{mm}\) f/1.2 lenses arranged front- to- front) focused 400- \(600\mu \mathrm{m}\) below the cortical surface. Imaging was restricted to a region of interest (ROI) as shown (red outline) overlaid on a photograph of the surface vasculature in Figure 1B. Increased blood flow indicative of neural activity was manifest as a reduction in cortical reflectance (and thereby image intensity).

Cortical activity was imaged during multiple presentations (trials) of stimulated and control (unstimulated) conditions, presented in random order. Within each trial, images were acquired continuously at a rate of \(5\mathrm{Hz}\) for a period of \(10\mathrm{s}\) . In the stimulated condition, electrical stimuli were presented for \(3\mathrm{s}\) beginning \(1\mathrm{s}\) after the beginning of image acquisition. Each trial was followed by a rest period of \(3\mathrm{s}\) (minimum) duration.

### E. Linear Model Analysis

The raw image sequences were first low- pass filtered in the spatial domain with a Gaussian smoothing function. The band- width of the Gaussian kernel ( \(\sigma_0 \approx 50\mu \mathrm{m}\) ) was chosen to be substantially less than the intrinsic low- pass point spread function of the imaging optics, estimated to be as much as \(250\mu \mathrm{m}\) full width at half maximum [6]. This spatial smoothing is therefore unlikely to have any deleterious effect

on the functional signal contained in the imaging data but does reduce high- frequency sensor noise.

After spatial filtering, slow changes in baseline cortical reflectance were removed by subtracting the mean of the first 5 frames of each image sequence from each image in the sequence. The resulting difference images were then trial averaged and all further analysis was performed on the average difference image sequences.

To identify regions of stimulus evoked cortical activity the time series for each pixel were analyzed independently using a generalized linear model (GLM) analysis similar to that described by Mayhew et al. [7]. In brief, the GLM method decomposes the time series data into a weighted sum of temporal basis functions using the method of least squares. This is expressed in compact form as follows,

\[y = B\cdot x + e \quad (1)\]

where \(y\) is a column vector containing the time series data, \(B\) , the design matrix, contains one column for each basis function and as many rows as there are samples in the time series data, \(x\) is a column vector of weights or parameters to be estimated and \(e\) is a column vector of errors.

We employed four temporal basis functions as illustrated in Figure 2A: a gamma function (thick line; \(k = 7\) , \(\theta = 1\) ) representing a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF), unit sine and cosine functions with a nominal frequency of \(0.1\mathrm{Hz}\) , and a linear ramp. The HRF models the stimulus evoked response of interest. The unit sine and cosine functions are used to estimate the amplitude and phase of a slow oscillation evident in the trial averaged time series. This oscillation in the time series data likely reflects remnant cardiac or respiratory artifacts not entirely mitigated by trial averaging. It may also reflect remnants of the so called \(V\) - signal, a spontaneous low frequency oscillation often observed in intrinsic signal imaging data [7]. The linear ramp approximates a slow increase in cortical reflectance over the course of approximately \(50\%\) of trials. This drift is likely due to intrusion of the tail of the hemodynamic response from a preceding stimulated trial.

The advantage of GLM analysis is that the resulting parameters may be scaled using the associated matrix of residuals to produce a \(t\) - distributed \(z\) - score, which may be used for statistical inference. Two dimensional images plotting the \(z\) - score for each pixel are referred to as statistical parameter maps (SPMs). A pixel is deemed to be significantly activated if its \(z\) - score exceeds a given threshold.

We calculated conservative thresholds for significance as outlined by Mayhew et al. [7]. Thresholds were corrected for multiple comparisons by scaling based on the confidence level, the number of pixels in the image (notionally, the number of "tests" being performed), and the band- width of the Gaussian kernel used for spatial filtering.

### B. Supra-choroidal Stimulating Electrode

The stimulating electrode employed here is rudimentary compared to those being developed for use in retinal implants. This electrode was designed to facilitate registration of the retinal locus of the stimulus with the window of observation for cortical imaging. This registration is complicated in our experimental model due to the supra- choroidal placement of the stimulating electrode - behind the pigment epithelium, the tapetum and the choroid. This prevents observation of the electrode position by conventional funduscopy as is routinely done for epiretinal electrode placement (e.g., Elfar et al. [8]). Assuming normative cortical magnification for the cat, our electrode separation at the retina amounts to a functional separation in the cortex of \(\sim 3 - 4 \mathrm{mm}\) . Given the size of our imaging window (see Figure 1B) this afforded an excellent chance of registering at least one electrode within the window whilst allowing a small possibility of registering two.

### C. Intrinsic Signal Imaging and Other Recording Modalities

Intrinsic signal imaging lacks sufficient temporal resolution to explore the temporal dynamics of cortical responses evoked by electrical stimulation of the retina (see for example the multi- modal spiking responses and local field potentials recorded by Elfar et al. [8]). Additional insight into the spatio- temporal cortical response may be obtained by combining intrinsic signal imaging with electrophysiological recordings. A particularly attractive possibility involves combining intrinsic signal imaging with simultaneous recordings of the underlying neuronal activity. To this end, we have recently fabricated optically transparent electrode arrays suitable for recording evoked cortical potentials concurrently with intrinsic signal imaging [9].

## V. CONCLUSION

Linear model analysis is seemingly under utilized in intrinsic signal imaging. Our experience suggests that GLM analysis represents an additional useful tool for the analysis of intrinsic signal imaging data and offers two notable advantages over conventional analysis techniques: providing an intuitive mechanism for mitigating confounding artifacts due to the underlying physiology or to experiment design, and providing a direct means of assessing the statistical significance of any observed change in cortical reflectance. Furthermore, our imaging results confirm that supra- choroidal electrical stimulation can elicit localized activity in the cortex consistent with focal activation of the retina and is therefore a viable site of intervention for a retinal prosthesis.
